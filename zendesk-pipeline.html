the zendesk support pipeline

375ai · october 2025 to july 2026

story

In October 2025 we publicly launched 375go, our mobile app. It passed 250,000 downloads worldwide, and the majority of users reported a pleasant experience. As the app scaled, so did our need for a smooth customer support experience.

The team had no channel to organize, understand and resolve support requests, so I built one before the volume made it urgent. I set up and administered Zendesk, wrote the knowledge base and answered the tickets myself. Then I automated the part that repeated.

how it works

I built the pipeline around Zendesk's own AI agent, the product that Answer Bot became. It answers with generative replies grounded in our knowledge base, so a reply comes from a document we wrote, not from the model's memory. The retrieval layer is Zendesk's. The knowledge base, the agent setup and the status flow were mine.

Three tools carry the work.

The knowledge base started with what we already had: FAQs, technical documentation, support articles, and how-to guides with documented steps for common issues.

The agent greeted the user, assessed the message, and replied with specific steps for the user's issue. If the issue persisted past that first reply, the agent asked for what an engineer would eventually need: device details, steps to reproduce, screenshots, videos. All of it went onto the ticket.

If the agent had no clear path forward, the status moved to needs human and looped me into the conversation.

I reviewed the ticket, asked for anything still missing, and set the status to eng review. Zapier's ticket-updated trigger, filtered on that status, pulled the ticket data and created a Linear issue with everything engineering needed: the user and device info, a description of the issue, the steps already tried, and the steps to reproduce it.

impact

In the first six months, more than 1,000 common issues were resolved by the agent with no human in the loop. Our community described support as diligent and helpful.

The pipeline saved me an estimated 20 to 25 hours of work every week, and enhanced the user experience and community morale.

what i learned

The fun part about building a system is figuring out how to remove yourself from it, or build as little reliance on your participation as possible. We are our own bottlenecks.

limit 1) keep costs down budget approval for one seat, and that is it.

limit 2) product the functionalities that would remove the barrier were paywalled.

what i could've explored

  • Swap my seat for one service identity and call the tickets api. This route is still pretty limited. The api updates the ticket thread with a comment written by the service account, which is not the same as replying to the user as support. It degrades the user experience.
  • Or a computer-use tool call with a skill that reproduces the manual work of passing messages between engineering and the user, with my account as the vehicle. Not as deterministic, and prone to random bugs, authentication expirations and other stop-the-process triggers.
