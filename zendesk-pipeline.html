the zendesk support pipeline

375ai · october 2025 to july 2026 · zendesk, zapier, linear

story

In October 2025 we publicly launched 375go, our mobile app. It passed 250,000 downloads worldwide, and with that came a wave of support requests: crashes, login problems, missing rewards, questions about how the app worked.

The team had no channel to organize, understand and resolve those requests. I set up and administered Zendesk for the company, and I answered the support tickets myself. One inbox, one person. I needed a way to handle the volume without answering every ticket by hand.

how it works

I built the pipeline around Zendesk's own AI agent, the product that Answer Bot became. It answers with generative replies grounded in our knowledge base, so a reply comes from a document we wrote, not from the model's memory. The retrieval layer is Zendesk's. The knowledge base, the agent setup and the status flow were mine.

Three tools carry the work.

The knowledge base started with what we already had: FAQs, technical documentation, support articles, and how-to guides with documented steps for common issues. No answer was added without a source document behind it.

Every inquiry got the same first pass. The agent greeted the user, assessed the message, and replied with specific steps for the user's issue. If the issue survived that first reply, the agent asked for what an engineer would eventually need: device details, steps to reproduce, screenshots, videos. All of it went onto the ticket.

A ticket changed hands on one condition: the agent had no clear path forward. Guesswork was not allowed, so the status moved to needs human and I joined the conversation.

I reviewed the ticket, asked for anything still missing, and set the status to eng review. Zapier's ticket-updated trigger, filtered on that status, pulled the ticket data and created a Linear issue with everything engineering needed: the user and device info, a description of the issue, the steps already tried, and the steps to reproduce it.

impact

In the first six months, more than 1,000 common issues were resolved by the agent with no human in the loop. Our community described support as diligent and helpful.

The pipeline saved me an estimated 20 to 25 hours of work every week. Tickets stopped piling up while I answered them one at a time.

what i learned

The bottleneck was me, not the automation. Two limits made me the only path between the engineers and the users, and only one of them was money.

The money limit was one paid agent seat on Suite Team, Zendesk's cheapest plan. Light agent seats, the cheaper tier, were an add-on on that plan. They cost less than full seats, but they were still a purchase.

The product limit is the part I did not understand well enough. A light agent can read a ticket and leave a private note, and nothing more. They cannot post a public reply. So with light seats bought, an engineer could write the fix and still not send it.

The feature built for this already existed, and I did not use it. Side conversations attach an email, Slack or Teams thread to a ticket, and the people on that thread do not need agent seats. It has been in the product for years. It needs Suite Professional or above, and we were on Team. That is the first thing I would ask for.

If the plan cannot move, light agent seats plus a small relay come next. Engineering works on the ticket, not over email, and the relay turns a private note into the public reply.

The API path comes next. Zendesk creates a comment when you update a ticket, and the author defaults to the account that authenticated the call. One service identity holds a seat and calls PUT /api/v2/tickets/{id} with a comment object, public set to true. The thread updates. Two rules: use a dedicated service account instead of my login, or the audit trail reads as me, and stay inside the rate limit, which is 200 requests a minute on Suite Team and 400 on Suite Growth.

MCP is the newest path. Zendesk runs an MCP server, and Bridge lets an outside agent act inside Zendesk through scoped tools, with a person confirming each change. It is in early access and the pricing is not settled, so I would test it rather than design against it.
